Part one · frameworkii.

Why in-situ simulation

In-situ simulation happens in the real clinical environment with real staff, on the unit where the care will actually be delivered. It is more powerful than centre-based simulation because the whole system gets stress-tested alongside the team. Missing equipment, broken pumps, unclear sightlines, ward culture, the bleep that no one answers — all are revealed in-situ in a way they never are in a purpose-built sim suite.

In-situ work is also more demanding to deliver. Real patients are nearby and faculty must protect them. The unit is busy and faculty time is contested. The environment cannot be controlled. These constraints are real and the faculty pack provides specific guidance for managing them.

The published evidence supports the approach. Walker and colleagues demonstrated a long-term reduction in mortality after multidisciplinary in-situ simulation training in a teaching hospital. The HSSIB and its predecessor have repeatedly named non-technical failures as contributors to preventable harm; in-situ work is uniquely placed to address them because it exposes the system as well as the team.

Why human factors

Clinical simulation tends to select for technical knowledge. Once a team has algorithm fluency, the next bottleneck is the soft tissue around the algorithm — leadership, communication, situational awareness, decision-making, task management and personal resource management. These are the domains identified across the ANTS, NOTSS and Medical NTS frameworks and within the Resuscitation Council UK crisis management curriculum.

They are also the domains repeatedly named in coroners' Regulation 28 reports. They cannot be reliably learned at the bedside because the cases are rare, the feedback is delayed, and the moments are often unrecognised at the time. Simulation is the only deliberate-practice route for these skills.

The layering principle

Each human factors variant uses an existing clinical scenario as its substrate. The clinical pathway is deliberately straightforward for the level of learner — the challenge is exclusively non-technical. If learners are stretched clinically and non-technically at the same time, the debrief becomes muddled and the HF lessons are lost in the clinical noise. Three rules govern the design:

  1. One human factors challenge per scenario. Stacking challenges produces chaos, not learning.
  2. Confederates work to a script. Improvisation makes debriefs incomparable across teams.
  3. Debrief the behaviour, not the bedside. The clinical decisions are a lens; the substrate is how the team made them.

The human factors toolkit

Plug-in challenges that can be applied to any clinical scenario. Twelve variants in Part Three demonstrate them in worked form.

  • Authority gradient — a senior is wrong and is reachable only by phone.
  • Assertiveness — a junior team member has information the leader needs and is reluctant to volunteer it.
  • Fixation error — the team locks onto a plausible but wrong diagnosis and a key feature is missed.
  • Cognitive load and parallel demands — interruptions, competing patients, bleeps, phones.
  • Equipment or resource failure — missing drug, broken kit, no porter, no bed.
  • Handover failure — the leader inherits a deteriorating patient mid-stream with poor information.
  • Family conflict — distressed or angry family present during active resuscitation.
  • Team conflict — a colleague openly disagrees with the plan in front of the patient.
  • Personal resource management — fatigue, hunger, a previous bad outcome that day.
  • System resource scarcity — the system cannot deliver what the patient needs in the available time.

The debrief framework

Debrief is where the learning happens. The scenario is the substrate. Plan twenty to twenty-five minutes; never run over thirty. Use two structures in sequence:

  1. Plus / Delta — five minutes. Surface-level reflection. What went well, what would you do differently. Vents the emotional load.
  2. Advocacy with inquiry — fifteen to twenty minutes. Focused on two or three specific moments. This is the deep work.

The advocacy-with-inquiry technique was developed at the Centre for Medical Simulation in Boston and codified by Rudolph and colleagues. The structure: state your observation, share your frame, inquire into the learner's frame, listen, respond. A worked example from the Speaking-Up variant:

I noticed that when the nurse mentioned the glucose at the start, you continued giving the second dose of lorazepam without acknowledging her. I'm wondering if you heard her, and if you did, what was going through your head at that moment?

The temptation is to skip to the answer. Resist. Behaviour change requires understanding the learner's mental model first; unless you understand it, your correction will not stick.

Annual schedule · monthly cadencevii.

Twelve months covers the full clinical estate and the full HF curriculum. A fortnightly cadence delivers the same programme in six months. Faculty teams running both modes are advised to alternate clinical and HF runs rather than running them consecutively, so that learners experience the HF challenge after they have already worked the clinical substrate.
